Hardening & Tempering

Heat, quench in oil, then temper back to the hardness the part needs. The temperature is set by the material.

Temperature
Set by grade. EN24 hardens at 840–860°C
Result
The hardness your drawing specifies

Every material has its own hardening temperature, so the cycle starts from the grade. Take EN24: it goes up to 840–860°C, holds until the whole section has reached temperature, and is then quenched in oil. The quench locks the steel into its hardest structure.

Straight out of the quench the part is too brittle to use. Tempering at a lower temperature brings it back to the hardness you asked for, and that number is what the tempering cycle is set to.

  • Hardening temperature set by grade
  • Oil quenching
  • Tempered to the hardness you ask for
  • Hardness checked before dispatch

Case Hardening & Tempering

A hard, wear-resistant case over a tough core. Carburised at 940°C, then hardened from 840°C.

Temperature
Carburise at 940°C, harden from 840°C
Result
1.5–2 mm case from a six-hour soak

Low-carbon steel like EN1A can’t take much hardness on its own, so carbon is added to the surface first. The parts are carburised at 940°C, and a six-hour soak builds a case about 1.5 to 2 mm deep.

For case hardening and tempering, the charge then comes down to 840°C, soaks for an hour and is quenched in oil or water, depending on the grade. When only the case is wanted, the parts cool inside the furnace and come out cold, ready for machining.

  • Carburised at 940°C
  • Six-hour soak for a 1.5–2 mm case
  • Hardened from 840°C, one-hour soak
  • Oil or water quench, or furnace cooled for case only

Annealing

Making a material softer so it machines and forms easily. Heated for its grade, then cooled in the furnace.

Temperature
Set by grade. 410 anneals at 880–900°C
Result
Softened and ready to machine

Annealing makes a material softer. Each grade has its own annealing temperature; 410 stainless, for example, goes to 880–900°C.

The part holds there until it is even all the way through, then cools slowly inside the furnace and only comes out once it has cooled. That slow cool is what leaves it soft.

  • Temperature set by grade
  • Soaked through the full section
  • Cooled inside the furnace
  • Taken out after cooling

Normalising & Tempering

The step after forging. Heated to 900–940°C, held for the section thickness, cooled in air.

Temperature
900–940°C, as the standard sets
Result
A refined, even grain after forging

Most of our normalising work arrives as forgings. The temperature depends on the material, and the standard puts most grades at 900–940°C.

Holding time comes from the thickness of the part. A 50 mm section holds for about two hours. After the soak the charge comes out and cools in air, and is tempered afterwards where the specification calls for it.

  • 900–940°C as per standard
  • Holding time set by section thickness
  • About two hours for a 50 mm section
  • Air cooled, then tempered

Stress Relieving

Mostly for welded components. Taking out the stress welding leaves behind, without changing the hardness.

Temperature
Set by the base material and the weld
Result
Stress relieved, hardness unchanged

Most of the stress relieving we do is on welded components. The base metal and the weld carry different stresses, and a part left that way can crack or move once it is machined or put into service.

A typical job is a cast valve body with weld deposited on top. The whole part is held at the temperature its base grade and welding procedure call for, then cooled slowly so the stress comes out evenly.

  • Welded components and fabrications
  • Base metal and weld relieved together
  • Temperature set by grade and welding procedure
  • Slow, even cooling

Solution Annealing

For non-magnetic stainless steels. Heated to 1040–1080°C, then quenched within 20 seconds.

Temperature
1040–1080°C, time by thickness
Result
Corrosion resistance restored, magnetism relieved

Solution annealing is for the non-magnetic stainless steels. The charge goes to 1040–1080°C and holds for a time set by the thickness, often around two hours.

Then speed matters. The parts go from the furnace into the liquid quench within 20 seconds. That fixes the structure, restores corrosion resistance and relieves the magnetism that welding or cold work can bring in.

  • 1040–1080°C
  • Soak time by thickness, around two hours
  • Into the quench within 20 seconds
  • Relieves induced magnetism

Age Hardening & Precipitation Hardening

For special grades like 718. After solution treatment, two long ageing holds bring out the strength.

Temperature
Two-step ageing, the second at 620°C
Result
Precipitation hardened to specification

Special grades such as 718 get their strength from precipitation, not from a quench. The material is solution treated first and aged afterwards.

Ageing is two long holds. The charge soaks for ten hours at the first ageing temperature, is furnace cooled to 620°C, holds there for eight hours and is then air cooled. Fine particles form inside the metal during those holds, and they are what bring the hardness up.

  • Solution treated first
  • Ten-hour soak at the first ageing step
  • Furnace cooled to 620°C, eight-hour hold
  • Air cooled

Stabilising

For the stabilised stainless grades F321 and F347, run to the cycle their specification sets.

Temperature
As the grade’s specification sets
Result
Protected against grain-boundary corrosion

F321 and F347 are stainless steels with titanium or niobium added to protect them from corrosion at the grain boundaries. A stabilising treatment is what puts that protection to work.

The parts are held to the cycle their specification calls for, so the titanium or niobium ties up the carbon before chromium can. The grain boundaries stay corrosion resistant, even in high-temperature service.

  • F321 and F347
  • Held to the specified cycle
  • Protects against grain-boundary corrosion
  • For high-temperature service
