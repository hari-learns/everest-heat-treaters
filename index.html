The scale we work on

Every degree is a different metal.

Below about 400°C steel does not glow — the colour you see is the oxide film, which is how toolmakers have judged tempering by eye for two centuries. Above it, the metal is incandescent. Drag through the range.

What we do

Eight processes, one job card.

Eight processes cover almost everything that comes through the door. If your drawing calls for something else, or does not specify at all, send it over — specifying the treatment is part of the job.

Hardening & Tempering

The core process. Heat into the austenitic range, quench, then temper back to the hardness the part actually needs.

Temperature
820–870°C, tempered 150–600°C
Result
25–62 HRC depending on grade and temper
Read the process

Case Carburising

A hard, wear-resistant skin over a core that stays tough. The standard treatment for gears and transmission parts.

Temperature
900–930°C
Result
58–62 HRC case, 0.2–1.5 mm effective depth
Read the process

Induction Hardening

Hardness exactly where it is needed and nowhere else. Seconds, not hours, and the rest of the part never gets hot.

Temperature
850–950°C at the surface only
Result
50–60 HRC, 1–5 mm depth
Read the process

Annealing

Softening. Taking work-hardened or previously treated material back to a machinable, stress-free state.

Temperature
650–900°C, slow cooled
Result
Softened, uniform, ready to machine or form
Read the process

Normalising

Grain refinement after forging or casting. The step that makes everything downstream predictable.

Temperature
870–950°C, air cooled
Result
Uniform fine grain, improved machinability
Read the process

Stress Relieving

Removing the locked-in stress from welding and machining, before it removes your tolerances.

Temperature
550–650°C
Result
Dimensional stability, no change to hardness

Proof

You cannot see heat treatment. So we measure it.

Heat treatment is invisible from the outside. A part that was never properly hardened looks exactly like one that was, right up until it fails in service. Everything below exists so you do not have to take our word for it.

Rockwell hardness

HRC and HRB on every batch, at the points the drawing specifies. The number goes on the report that travels with the parts.

Microhardness & case depth

Vickers traverse on a mounted, polished cross-section to measure effective and total case depth — the only honest way to verify a carburised or nitrided case.

Microstructure

Mounted, polished, etched and examined. Confirms the structure is what the process was supposed to produce, and catches retained austenite, decarburisation and grain growth.

Furnace survey & pyrometry

Temperature uniformity surveys and instrument calibration on schedule. A furnace that reads 30°C low will pass every visual check and fail every part.

Microstructure

The evidence is in the grain.

Etched cross-sections under the microscope — what the process actually did to the metal, rather than what the furnace chart claims.
